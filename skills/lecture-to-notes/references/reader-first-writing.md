# Reader-first writing for lecture notes

Use this guide after source extraction and before outlining. It adapts reader-first
writing principles to authored lecture notes. Preserve the lecture's evidence and voice;
do not make the PDF read like a cleaned transcript or an academic-paper template.

## 1. Protect the source before improving the prose

Record the following before drafting:

- speaker claims, examples, predictions, qualifications, and disagreements;
- equations, symbols, code, diagrams, numerical values, comparison conditions, and units;
- names, technical terms, timestamps, and any visible source attribution;
- boundaries between established background, the speaker's interpretation, and the
  note writer's synthesis.

Do not silently turn a forecast into a fact, an anecdote into general evidence, a
correlation into causality, or an editorial inference into the speaker's claim. Attribute
interpretive material with phrases such as “讲者认为”, “视频用……解释”, or “本讲义据此整理为”.
Use attribution where it protects the claim boundary, not at the start of every sentence.

## 2. Build the reader's argument map

Write one sentence for each question before choosing section titles:

1. What central question does the lecture answer?
2. What does the intended reader already know?
3. What should the reader be able to explain, calculate, compare, or decide afterward?
4. Which ideas must be understood first because later ideas depend on them?
5. What evidence supports each main answer, and where does that evidence stop?

Turn these answers into section questions. A section should exist because the reader needs
its answer, not because the transcript changed topic or the source crossed a five-minute
boundary. Reorder material when a later explanation supplies the prerequisite for an
earlier claim. Preserve timestamps in provenance artifacts even when prose order changes.

## 3. Turn speech into authored teaching prose

- Remove greetings, verbal fillers, repeated questions, false starts, and self-repairs.
- Combine scattered explanations when they answer the same reader question.
- Keep a concrete example close to the mechanism it explains.
- Preserve a useful disagreement or correction when it changes the concept, evidence, or
  boundary; omit conversational choreography that does not teach.
- Use the speaker's analogy only when it reduces cognitive load. Explain where the analogy
  stops if readers might overextend it.
- Do not add anecdotes, examples, motives, or background facts that are absent from the
  source unless they are verified and explicitly presented as added context.

## 4. Give paragraphs one main job

A useful teaching paragraph often performs some of these moves:

1. state the answer or claim;
2. explain the mechanism or reasoning;
3. show source evidence or a worked example;
4. state the consequence, decision rule, or limitation.

Do not force all four moves into every paragraph. Split a paragraph when it defines a
concept, derives a formula, reports several results, and adds a caveat at once. Join short
paragraphs when they merely repeat one thought in different words.

Check every sentence handoff. The first sentence should prepare the concept introduced by
the next. End a paragraph with its result or consequence rather than a generic statement
that the topic is “important”.

## 5. Write concrete, natural technical Chinese

- State the positive proposition directly. Keep “不是……而是……” only when the contrast
  prevents a real misunderstanding.
- Replace “值得注意的是”, “这一点非常重要”, and similar announcements with the
  consequence that matters.
- Introduce plain meaning before an acronym, project label, stage number, or internal name.
- Prefer explicit subjects and concrete verbs. Explain a mechanism by what it allows,
  changes, or prevents.
- Review Chinese sentences above roughly 90 characters for stacked clauses. This is a
  review signal, not a ban on long sentences.
- Remove unsupported adjectives such as “全面”, “系统”, “显著”, “革命性”, or
  “鲁棒” unless the source defines or measures them.
- Vary paragraph and sentence rhythm naturally. Do not manufacture a “human voice” with
  casual asides, first-person anecdotes, or ornamental metaphors.
- Avoid repeated openings such as “首先/其次/最后”, “此外”, “因此”, and “这意味着”
  when they only decorate the transition. Keep them when they express a real sequence or
  causal relation.

## 6. Frame evidence so readers can interpret it

For every headline number, make clear:

1. what was measured or asserted;
2. compared with what;
3. under which sample, time, unit, or operating condition;
4. why the difference matters.

Place detailed values in tables when the lecture compares three or more alternatives.
Use the body to interpret a figure; keep the caption concise enough to identify the object,
comparison, and scope. Mention a figure or table in the prose before it appears.

For a speaker's market, policy, or future prediction, retain its reasoning and uncertainty.
Do not create a numerical model merely to make the discussion appear technical.

## 7. Open and close sections for the reader

Open each major section with the reader question or its short answer. Introduce the plain
idea before implementation detail, notation, evaluation machinery, or exceptions.

Use `本章小结` to answer the section question in fresh, compressed language. Do not copy
the opening, enumerate every subsection again, or end with “综上所述，本章十分重要”. When
the next section depends on the current result, close with that dependency as a natural
handoff.

The final synthesis should:

- state what the lecture enables the reader to understand or do;
- connect mechanisms across sections;
- separate the speaker's conclusion from the note writer's compression;
- name the demonstrated boundary and a concrete next question.

## 8. Revise in separate passes

Run these passes after the complete draft exists:

1. **Structure:** prerequisites precede dependent ideas; each section answers one question.
2. **Argument:** each paragraph has one main job; evidence is followed by meaning.
3. **Source voice:** facts, speaker judgments, quotations, and editorial synthesis remain distinct.
4. **Terminology:** terms are defined once in reader language and then used consistently.
5. **Sentence flow:** remove oral debris, vague subjects, stacked clauses, mechanical
   contrast, and repeated paragraph templates.
6. **Evidence:** recheck every number, qualifier, formula, label, and causal statement.
7. **Rendered reading:** extract text from the compiled PDF, read it continuously, and
   inspect changed pages at final size.

An automated phrase search can identify candidates; it cannot pass the prose gate. Do not
rewrite an accurate sentence merely because it is passive, long, metaphorical, or uses a
question heading. Change it only when meaning, logic, emphasis, or readability improves.

## Final reader-first checklist

- Can a first-time reader state the central question after the opening page?
- Does each section answer a concrete question before giving details?
- Are technical labels introduced after their plain meaning?
- Can every number and strong causal claim be traced to source evidence?
- Are speaker judgments visibly separate from added synthesis?
- Does each paragraph advance the explanation rather than restate it?
- Do transitions follow the logic rather than a fixed template?
- Did density gates add teaching content rather than prose or visual filler?
- Does the conclusion explain value and boundary without replaying the whole document?
